## Question

How can the same newborn human organism become, depending on its developmental
embedding, a non-linguistic survivor of extreme deprivation, an ordinary social
adult, or a research-level reflective agent? What developmental architecture
lets one starting biology support such divergent adult personhood?

## Claim

Adult cognitive personhood is bootstrapped, not unfolded and not inscribed. A
small set of biologically prepared proto-agential capacities is sufficient to
enter a structured social environment as a participant; participation generates
the learning data that supports the next capacity, which changes how the child
participates, and so on recursively. Caregivers first run, from outside,
regulatory and symbolic operations the child cannot yet perform (affect
co-regulation, attention direction, emotional labelling, norm enforcement,
memory co-authoring, executive scaffolding), and across years the child
internalizes them. Language reorganizes the architecture into a recursive one;
norms make action answerable; cumulative culture installs the institutional and
reflective layers no individual could generate alone. The account is
developmental-cognitive and is held strictly separate from moral or legal
status: the newborn lacks adult-style cognitive personhood and retains full
moral standing. The strongest confirmation is the pattern of sparing and loss
under deprivation, which is what a layered, experience-expectant, recursive
system predicts and what neither pure nativism nor pure environmentalism
predicts cleanly.

## Cornerstone literature

A synthesis that omits a field's foundational authors is not yet a synthesis.
The names this paper must engage, all now present in sources.md:

- **Attachment / co-regulation:** Bowlby (1969); Ainsworth et al. (1978); De
  Wolff & van IJzendoorn (1997, the sensitivity-security meta-analysis);
  Taipale (2016); Atzil et al. (2018, allostatic co-regulation).
- **Vygotskian internalization:** Vygotsky (1978, 1987); Wood, Bruner & Ross
  (1976, scaffolding); Winsler, Fernyhough & Montero (2009, private speech).
- **Shared intentionality / cultural learning:** Tomasello (1999, 2019);
  Tomasello et al. (2005); Tomasello, Kruger & Ratner (1993); Csibra & Gergely
  (2009); Trevarthen & Aitken (2001); Liszkowski et al. (2004).
- **Autobiographical / narrative self:** Nelson (1996); Nelson & Fivush (2004);
  Reese, Haden & Fivush (1993); Gallagher (2000, minimal vs narrative self).
- **Development of the person / conscience:** Thompson (2006); Rakoczy,
  Warneken & Tomasello (2008); Lewis et al. (1989, self-conscious emotions).
- **Predictive processing / active inference:** Friston (2010); Clark (2013);
  Köster et al. (2020); Cittern et al. (2018, attachment under active inference).
- **Language acquisition / critical period:** Mayberry & Lock (2003); Hall
  (2017); Kuhl, Tsao & Liu (2003); Saffran, Aslin & Newport (1996).
- **Deprivation evidence:** Nelson et al. (2007, BEIP) and follow-ups (Bos et
  al. 2009; Vanderwert et al. 2010); Sonuga-Barke et al. (2017, ERA); Curtiss
  (1977, Genie); Candland (1993, feral-child caution); Greenough, Black &
  Wallace (1987, experience-expectant development).
- **Cumulative culture:** Henrich (2016); Boyd & Richerson (1985); Tennie, Call
  & Tomasello (2009); Lyons, Young & Keil (2007, overimitation).
- **Person as historically assembled category:** Mauss (1985).
